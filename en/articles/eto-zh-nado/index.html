Well, Would You Look at That!

Elena Berezina

In the cosmos, the only observable phenomena are “events”. An observer does not register “celestial bodies”, stars, planets, or assorted asteroids, but rather events. Because we Earthlings detect light — emitted or reflected (from primary or secondary sources, respectively) — from point sources (and in space all sources are point sources). And so far, Earthlings are the only known recipients.

Light travels from an event (another flash, an emission of photons) to an observer. Along the shortest path, in a straight line. Distance is measured in light-travel time — the time photons need to cover that distance. And we always patiently wait for light from afar to reach the rods and cones of our retinas. As the Poet observed, even the light of stars long since dead reaches us sooner or later. For example, some fourteen billion years (give or take) have passed for us to see light from the farthest reaches of the observable universe. It's all very simple.

But what is stranger, what is most incomprehensible, is how the sapiens who so proudly bear that name manage... to overlook the change in the sign of time when the direction of motion through space is reversed! Imagine that — in chorus (not boys, but learned grown men!) they proclaim a “positive arrow of time”! Whether our little seven-colored flower of light is flying from the event to the observer or the other way around. Yet if motion in one direction (toward us) is positive, motion in the opposite direction certainly is not! Negative time is the most natural, self-evident phenomenon, which scientists somehow manage (or prefer) not to see right in front of them. Not all of them, but the majority, along with the broad masses (who are ignorant, but may be forgiven).

And there is nobody to set these sapiens straight: the crowning glory of creation. Scholars, they be!

All that is required is to realize that if light travels from point A to point B, from the past into the present, then motion from B to A takes place in the opposite direction through space-time. Through the space-time continuum. In other words, when a cosmonaut sets off for distant stars, he flies into the past. “Let's go!” he shouts. “I want a second-class train ticket back to my childhood!” he yells.

That is why the cosmonaut's time contracts each time, acquiring a “negative increment”.

Because my eyesight has deteriorated, I cannot find a quotation from a book by Highfield and Coveney in which these specialists mention that, when we fly away into space, we are always moving into the past — at a modest speed, very different from the speed of light, but still... So take my word for it. Unlike the optics of my eyes, my brain's analytical apparatus is in excellent working order, regardless of what you may think about it.
