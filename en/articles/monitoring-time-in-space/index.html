Monitoring Time in Space

Elena Berezina

“The use of the term ‘space-time’ stems from a poverty of terminology. We might just as well call water ‘hydrogen–oxygen’” (M. Kamenarovich).

“Where nothing happens, there is no time. For time is not a quantity introduced into a system from outside, but an immanent property of the world, connected with the nature of the changes taking place within it.

There may be several times moving in different directions. Some are reversible, others are not.

From the observer’s point of view… only one time flows, of course; this happens because the observer measures time by their own clock, because they have immersed all the diverse streams of time in the single time given to them by Nature” (Stanisław Lem).

All classical scientific theories claimed to describe the world objectively, but did not take into account the observer’s influence on the object of knowledge. The new physics is called upon to abandon this underestimation of the observer’s role without denying the possibility of obtaining objective knowledge.

Both relativity and quantum mechanics have emphatically demonstrated the need for such an approach.

To retain objectivity, we must clearly establish the circumstances in which the observer assesses incoming information, understand the reasons for the involuntary limitations of our assessment, and foresee their consequences.

For example, an observer can see only emitted or reflected light, at the moment photons reach the receptors of their eye (auxiliary optics do not alter the principle).

Photons need some time to reach the observer, so any emission of particles occurs before it is detected.

That is, photons move from the “event” to the observer in ordinary positive time—from the past to the future.

It is precisely positive time that today’s official science, with the exception of a few outstanding scientists, regards as the only real time.

Nevertheless, let us try to examine the situation more deeply.

Clearly, if the emission of photons is taken as the initial, zero moment of time, then, as the particles move toward the observer, their time coordinates will take on positive values of increasing magnitude.

But if, conversely, the reference point (“time zero”) is associated with the observer’s location, it is logical to conclude that time must take on negative values whose magnitude is greater the farther the moving point is from the observer in space.

One can, of course, also measure from any “nonzero” point, bearing in mind that motion away from the observer always takes place in “reverse” time, from the future to the past.

Backward motion in time can occur either with a decrease in the magnitude of positive numbers or with an increase in the magnitude of negative numbers, depending on the coordinates of the starting point. When moving from the event to the observer, however, as already indicated above, the changes correspond, conversely, to an increase in the magnitude of positive values or a decrease in the magnitude of negative ones.

Moreover, this applies to any subluminal observer, wherever they may be, which supports both the isotropy of the universe and the objective nature of the knowledge obtained.

The speed of motion plays only an auxiliary role, in the sense that the decrease or increase in the absolute values of time readings occurs more rapidly at a higher speed.

When the observed object moves at a subluminal speed, a negative increment in magnitude makes it possible to register only a compression, a slowing of time, which nevertheless retains positive values.

The unobservability of the reverse motion of photons (away from the recipient) and, consequently, the “elusiveness” of genuine negative time have given grounds for speaking of a supposedly unidirectional “arrow of time” in the Universe, since our world of positive mass is a world of subluminal speeds.

But already at the speed of light (characteristic of energy-carrying particles), the negative increment “cancels out” the entire course of positive time. The time of photons and other particles travelling at the speed of light is zeroed out. The particle acquires wave properties and, as it were, is “multiplied” into several places at once, providing a logical explanation of the quantum–wave duality of particles.

Superluminal objects, however, which are inaccessible to visual observation, acquire a kind of “overshoot” of reverse time thanks to their speed beyond the limit. As a result, they are constantly in genuine NEGATIVE (“minus”) time, regardless of their direction of motion.

Figuratively speaking, the superluminal “traveller” becomes not only “younger than their brother”, but younger than their own self, entering their own past.

The world of superluminal speeds is a world of “cosmic invisibles”, a world of inverted cause-and-effect relationships.

In quantum mechanics, antiparticles exist in reverse (negative) time (the rule of CPT symmetry).

The “dark matter” of the Universe apparently consists of antimatter as well.

That is, if antimatter has “disappeared”, it has done so only from our field of view, which makes any talk of a violation of the law of conservation entirely meaningless.

Human beings cannot perceive the “symmetrical” world through their senses, but this by no means implies that such a world is unreal: indirect evidence and the logic of things unequivocally support its existence.

It should also be understood that the concept of negative time does not refute relativity, but develops and improves it. Relativity becomes a special case of a new generalized theory that makes it possible to resolve many of the unsolved problems of modern physical science—the very problems whose solutions have long been “in the air”.
