The Principle of Causality — a Reason to Stand on Principle

Elena Berezina

There is no doubt that acknowledging the existence, alongside the material universe, of a world made of antimatter, with time running backward, is in itself tantamount to acknowledging that a signal can propagate faster than light.

Many mistakenly believe that the Theory of Relativity prohibits superluminal speeds. This is entirely untrue.

The prohibition on motion faster than light comes neither from special relativity nor from the mathematical calculations of Maxwell or Lorentz, but solely from… the principle of causality. Einstein himself, considering the question of superluminal signal transmission, wrote:

“WE ARE COMPELLED TO CONSIDER POSSIBLE A MECHANISM OF SIGNAL TRANSMISSION IN WHICH THE RESULTING EFFECT PRECEDES ITS CAUSE.”

And further: “Although this result contains no logical contradictions, it nevertheless conflicts so strongly with the character of all our experience that the impossibility of the assumption V > C appears sufficiently justified.”

Notice that after the word “although”, the discussion concerns nothing more than everyday experience, which once provided the basis for notions of a flat Earth and has no direct bearing on science in general or relativity in particular.

That is, it is rather this very statement by Einstein that contradicts special relativity, whereas the theory itself, on the contrary, “compels” us to acknowledge the possibility of superluminal speeds.

Yet the notion that a superluminal signal speed supposedly contradicts relativity has become so deeply rooted that even L. Wong, who conducted an impressive experiment in which the speed of light was exceeded by a factor of 300(!) under laboratory conditions, believes that this experiment… is inconsistent with relativity!

At the same time, the scientist reasonably emphasizes that the claim that superluminal speeds are unattainable apparently applies only to objects with a rest mass greater than zero.

“Light, however, can be represented either as waves, to which the concept of mass does not apply at all, or as photons with a rest mass known to be zero,” Wong writes, “so the speed of light in a vacuum is by no means the limit.”

Let us note in passing that, strictly speaking, a photon is never “at rest” at all.

So, we need to separate the issues here.

First of all, we must understand that the principle of causality, which breaks down at and above the speed of light, has a domain of applicability confined to the world of matter.

It does not apply to the field—energy-carrying particles—since massless particles, upon reaching the speed of light, can be in many places at once, in accordance with wave properties, and can serve as their own antiparticles.

Still less can it apply to objects made of antimatter, which invariably exist in reversed time, where entropy, a function of time, does not increase as it does in the world of matter, but decreases. Consequently, processes are reversible, and it is precisely this reversibility that allows an effect to precede its cause!

So, let me repeat: the principle of causality is unshakable for the material (visible) universe. But the world of antimatter is precisely the environment that allows superluminal speeds to become a physical reality. In the words of antihydrogen researchers, it is “a key to another universe, where time reverses its direction—from the future to the past—everything left becomes right, and everything positive becomes negative”.

Indeed, one need only know that the masses of a particle and an antiparticle can annihilate each other to understand that these masses differ fundamentally in some respect. And at least at the moment of annihilation, they can hardly have the same sign. Otherwise, they would add together rather than cancel each other out.

It seems that nature contains a single fundamental entity that changes so profoundly with its speed of motion that it takes on different signs—from positive, through zero, to imaginary and negative values (alternatively, the square of an imaginary quantity)—penetrating another space-time dimension.

Since we, inhabitants of an exclusively material world, have only a positive “measuring stick”, we are inclined to declare it “ordinary positive mass” if and when we contrive to capture and measure it, thereby violating the rule of CPT symmetry, as a result of which… the first law of thermodynamics appears to be violated(!). And we like to repeat that the antiparticle’s “ordinary positive mass” is an “established fact”. Yet even a particle’s mass at rest is an elastic concept, since, strictly speaking, a particle, too, does not exist at rest.

As Einstein wittily remarked, if a fact contradicts the theory, so much the worse for the fact.

Still, anyone can make a mistake in the finer points of interpretation by yielding to the weight of conventional wisdom.

What matters is that the scientific method allows people to perceive the world around them as accurately as possible and to correct their mistakes as new knowledge accumulates.

Today, when even the latest radio-wave studies by astrophysicists give grounds for suspecting that certain cosmic objects move at superluminal speeds, and an impressive number of experiments both slowing down and speeding up light have been conducted in laboratories around the world, we can be confident that Einstein, with his remarkable ability to think unconventionally, would no longer make such a sweeping statement about the inadmissibility of superluminal speeds, which his own theory “compels us to discuss”.

As for those who take an excessively principled stand on preserving the inviolability of outdated postulates, without understanding the essence of relativity, and wish to be more Catholic than the Pope… well, we must admit that they are no Einsteins. Unfortunately.
